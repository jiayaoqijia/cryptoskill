# Level versus change

## Standard

A level answers where something is; a change answers how fast it is moving; a second difference answers whether the movement is accelerating. Each is a different claim, and most macro disagreements are really disagreements about which one is being discussed. An inflation rate of three percent is a change; the price index is a level; the change in the change is what a central bank watches when it says inflation is cooling.

Base effects make the change misleading at predictable moments. A monthly change measured against an unusually high or low month a year earlier will look dramatic or flat for reasons that have nothing to do with the present. The honest reading states the level, the change, and the base effect together.

Primary sources: <https://fred.stlouisfed.org/> and <https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing>.

## Decision rules

- State which quantity is being discussed: level, change, or the change in the change.
- Compute the year-over-year and month-over-month changes and present both where relevant.
- Identify the base month and whether it was unusual.
- Record the level alongside the change so the reader can see the starting point.
- Do not describe an acceleration from a single month's change.
- Where a series is seasonal, use the seasonally adjusted version and say so.

## Procedure

1. State the quantity: level, change, or second difference.
2. Compute the relevant changes and show the arithmetic.
3. Identify the base month and its unusualness.
4. Record the level beside the change.
5. Check seasonality and the adjustment used.
6. Write the reading with all three visible.

## Anti-patterns

- Quoting a change without the level.
- Treating a base effect as a trend.
- Describing acceleration from one month.
- Mixing seasonally adjusted and unadjusted figures.
- Comparing changes across different frequencies.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Quantity stated.

- [ ] Base month assessed.
- [ ] Level recorded beside the change.
- [ ] Seasonality checked and noted.
