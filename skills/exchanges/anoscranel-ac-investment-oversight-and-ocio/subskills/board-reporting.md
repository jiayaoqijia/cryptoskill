# Board reporting

## Standard

A board report exists to support a decision, and most of them support none because they report everything. The test is whether a reader can answer three questions from the first page: are we inside policy, are we on track for the objective, and is there anything that requires a decision now. A report that requires reading twenty pages to answer those has failed regardless of how complete it is.

The omission is as deliberate as the inclusion. Detailed attribution, holdings lists, and risk decompositions belong in an appendix that is available and not presented, because putting them in the main body displaces the three answers. The record should state what the report contains at each level and which decision each level is meant to support.

Primary sources: <https://www.cfainstitute.org/> and <https://www.sec.gov/investor>.

## Decision rules

- Record the decisions the report is meant to support.
- Record the first-page content: policy status, objective progress, items requiring decision.
- Record what is in the appendix and what is omitted entirely.
- Record the period covered and the comparison basis.
- Record the thresholds that would trigger an escalated report.
- Record who receives which level of the report.

## Procedure

1. Record the decisions the report supports.
2. Design the first page around those decisions.
3. Record the appendix contents.
4. Record the comparison basis and period.
5. Set escalation thresholds.
6. Review the report against the decisions it actually produced.

## Anti-patterns

- Reporting everything and deciding nothing.
- Putting attribution in the main body and the decision on page twenty.
- Using a comparison basis that flatters the period.
- Omitting a breach because it was resolved.
- Reporting to everyone at the same level of detail.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Decisions recorded.
- [ ] First-page content defined.
- [ ] Appendix contents recorded.
- [ ] Comparison basis recorded.
- [ ] Escalation thresholds set.
- [ ] Report reviewed against decisions produced.

The comparison basis deserves a line of its own because it is where reporting is most often steered without anyone intending it: measuring against a peer group chosen this quarter, or against a benchmark the provider selected, changes the story on the first page more than any figure in the appendix, and the basis should be fixed by policy rather than by the preparer.
