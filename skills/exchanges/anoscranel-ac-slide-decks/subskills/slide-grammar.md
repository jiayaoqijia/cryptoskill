# Slide Grammar

## Standard

- **Alley, assertion-evidence**: the headline is a full-sentence assertion; the body shows evidence (graphs, images, numbers), not bullet strings. Controlled studies with engineering audiences found comprehension gains over topic-headline slides. <https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-1-4419-8279-7>
- **One claim per slide**: the slide is the unit of argument. ⚠️ practitioner convention codified by Alley and Duarte, not a formal standard.
- **Headlines as a navigable argument**: reading only the headlines reproduces the deck's reasoning. <https://www.duarte.com/slideology/>
- **IBCS SAY rule**: state the message the visualization shows. The title says what the viewer should conclude. <https://www.ibcs.com/standards/>

## Decision rules

1. If the headline is a topic label, rewrite it as the conclusion: "Results" becomes "Throughput doubled after the switch".
2. If a slide carries two claims, split it. Slide count is cheap; a divided audience is not.
3. If the evidence does not visibly support the assertion, change the assertion or the evidence. Never patch both halfway.
4. Keep headlines to two lines maximum. ⚠️ A 12-to-18-word ceiling is a practitioner convention.
5. If the body is paragraph prose, convert it to evidence: numbers, an image, or a chart.
6. A purely navigational slide (divider, appendix marker) may carry a label. Mark it as an exception.
7. Words on the slide are for the audience, not the speaker. Speaker cues never appear on the slide.

## Procedure

1. List the intended claim of each slide in the outline.
2. Rewrite each claim as one sentence with subject, verb, and a number where a number exists.
3. Pair each headline with its evidence type: chart, image, table, or quote.
4. Delete or demote evidence that does not support its headline.
5. Skim the headlines only. Fix every gap in the argument chain.
6. Mark navigational slides as exceptions and drop them from the skim test.
7. Read each slide as a pair (headline, evidence). If the pair needs a speaker to connect it, rewrite the headline.

## Anti-patterns

- Topic-label headlines such as "Financials".
- Bullets carrying the argument while the headline idles.
- Two claims stapled into one headline with "and".
- Decorative evidence: charts or photos that argue nothing.
- A slide that only makes sense when read aloud.
- Headlines phrased as questions. The slide answers; it does not ask.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Each slide carries exactly one claim.
- [ ] Every content headline is a full-sentence assertion.
- [ ] The evidence visibly supports its headline.
- [ ] A headlines-only skim reproduces the argument.
- [ ] Navigational exceptions are marked.
- [ ] No speaker cues sit on the slides.
