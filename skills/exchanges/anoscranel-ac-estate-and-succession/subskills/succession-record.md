# Succession record

## Standard

The succession record is the single document that answers, for every asset, how it passes, who receives it, what documents are needed, and where the evidence is. It is written for the owner — to make the plan visible — and for the executor, to make it actionable. Its sections mirror the leaves: designations, titling, liquidity, account succession, and the executor checklist.

The record also carries the questions left open. Succession involves law and tax in the jurisdiction, and a record that pretends to resolve them is a record that will be relied on wrongly. Naming the professional questions, and the person who will answer them, is part of the plan.

Primary sources: <https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing> and <https://osf.io/prereg/>.

## Decision rules

- Every asset appears once, with title, mechanism, designee, and location of documents.
- Conflicts between designations and the will are recorded, not hidden.
- The liquidity section states the coverage calculation and the first-sale assets.
- The open questions are listed with the professional who will answer them.
- Review triggers are dated: family change, account change, jurisdiction change, annual review.
- The record is stored where the owner and the executor can both find it.

## Procedure

1. Assemble the sections from the leaves.
2. Verify each asset appears exactly once.
3. Record conflicts and open questions.
4. Set the review triggers.
5. Store the record and tell the executor where it is.
6. Review and re-date annually.

## Anti-patterns

- Recording assets twice under different sections.
- Hiding a conflict to avoid a difficult conversation.
- Leaving open questions unassigned.
- Storing the record only on the owner's device.
- No annual review.

## Done checklist

- [ ] All assets recorded once with full detail.
- [ ] Conflicts recorded.
- [ ] Liquidity section includes the coverage calculation.
- [ ] Open questions assigned.
- [ ] Review triggers dated.
- [ ] Stored and executor informed.

A record that is written once and reviewed annually is worth more than a perfect one written after a crisis: the annual review is where stale designations and changed titles are caught, and where the executor is reminded that the document exists.

Keeping the record dated and versioned means a reviewer can see which decisions were made when, and a change in family circumstances can be matched against the version that preceded it.
