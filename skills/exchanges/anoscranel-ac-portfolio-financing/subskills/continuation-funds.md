# Continuation funds

## Standard

A continuation fund buys assets from an expiring fund and holds them in a new vehicle with a new term and often new capital. Existing investors are offered a choice: roll their interest into the new vehicle, or take cash at a price set by the transaction. New investors supply the capital that funds the cash election.

The structure exists because some assets need longer than the original fund's term, and it creates a transaction in which the seller and the buyer are both controlled by the same sponsor. That is the conflict, and the protections around it — an independent valuation, an advisory committee approval, and a disclosure of the sponsor's own rollover — determine whether the price is a fair one.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://www.cfainstitute.org/>.

## Decision rules

- Record the assets being transferred and their original cost and holding period.
- Record the price offered for the cash election and the valuation supporting it.
- Record the sponsor's own election and its size.
- Record the new vehicle's terms: fees, term, and carried interest reset.
- Record the approval process and whether the advisory committee was independent.
- Record the deadline for the election and the consequences of not electing.

## Procedure

1. Record the assets and their history.
2. Record the price and the valuation basis.
3. Record the sponsor's rollover.
4. Record the new vehicle's terms.
5. Record approvals and their independence.
6. Write the transaction summary with the conflict named.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating the offered price as an independent valuation.
- Ignoring a carried interest reset that charges fees twice on the same assets.
- Overlooking the default election if no choice is made.
- Assuming the advisory committee's approval implies independence.
- Comparing the cash election with the rolling option without the new terms.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Assets and history recorded.
- [ ] Price and valuation recorded.
- [ ] Sponsor rollover recorded.
- [ ] New terms recorded.
- [ ] Approvals recorded.
- [ ] Conflict named in the summary.

The election mechanics carry more weight than the headline choice: a default that rolls an investor who does not respond, combined with a short window, converts inaction into consent, and an investor who assumed the cash election was automatic may find itself in a new ten-year vehicle. Recording the default beside the deadline is the single most practical step in reading these transactions.
