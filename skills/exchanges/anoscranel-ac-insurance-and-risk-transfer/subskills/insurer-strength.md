# Insurer strength

## Standard

Insurance is a promise, and the promise is only as good as the entity behind it. Claims-paying ability depends on capital, reserves, reinsurance arrangements, and the regulatory regime; the simplest public proxies are the financial-strength ratings and the insurer's statutory filings. None is a guarantee, and all of them can change over the policy's life.

The counterparty point is easy to forget because insurance feels like a utility. It is a counterparty with a multi-year obligation, and the assessment should be recorded at the same time as the coverage decision, not discovered at the claim.

Primary sources: <https://www.naic.org/> and <https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing>.

## Decision rules

- Record the insurer's financial-strength ratings with their date and agency.
- For large obligations, check the regulatory regime of the domicile.
- Note the role of reinsurance where disclosed, and the exposure if a reinsurer fails.
- Record the guaranty-association coverage limits of the relevant jurisdiction.
- Re-check the rating at each policy renewal.
- Where the insurer's disclosure is thin, cap the confidence.

## Procedure

1. Identify the underwriting entity, not the brand, and its domicile.
2. Pull financial-strength ratings with dates.
3. Check regulatory filings for capital and reserves where public.
4. Note reinsurance arrangements where disclosed.
5. Record guaranty-association limits for the jurisdiction.
6. Record the assessment with confidence and a review date.

## Anti-patterns

- Assessing the brand rather than the underwriting entity.
- Using a single rating without its date.
- Assuming guaranty associations cover the full amount.
- Ignoring reinsurance concentration.
- Skipping the re-check at renewal.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Underwriting entity and domicile identified.
- [ ] Ratings recorded with agencies and dates.
- [ ] Capital and reserves checked where public.
- [ ] Reinsurance noted.
- [ ] Guaranty limits recorded.
- [ ] Assessment and review date written.

The rating is a starting point rather than a conclusion: it describes the entity at a date, and the policy is a multi-year claim on that entity. Recording the date alongside the rating is what allows a later reader to judge whether the assessment is current or merely present.

For a household the practical threshold is simple: if the obligation would be a material loss when unpaid, the assessment must be current and recorded; if it would be trivial, a dated rating is sufficient and the attention belongs elsewhere.
