# Shareholder activism

## Overview

An activist campaign is a negotiation conducted in public, with a vote as the fallback. The activist accumulates a stake, files a disclosure that reveals the position and its purpose, makes demands, and either settles — usually for board seats and an agreement about what the company will do — or takes the question to shareholders in a proxy contest. The mechanics decide the outcome as much as the argument does.

This skill covers those mechanics: the filing that starts the clock, the settlement terms that end most campaigns, the vote arithmetic that decides the rest, and the economics that determine whether a campaign is worth running. It does not judge whether an activist is right, and it does not recommend a position in a contested company.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://www.cfainstitute.org/>.

## When to use

- When a 13D or an activist stake has been disclosed in a company.
- When a settlement agreement's terms need reading.
- When a proxy contest's vote arithmetic is being assessed.
- When a shareholder proposal or say-on-pay vote is on the ballot.

## When NOT to use

- For reading disclosed institutional positions generally (use ac-institutional-positioning).
- For deal spreads and event-driven positions (use ac-special-situations).
- For the company's investment case (use ac-equity-research).

## Status and honest limits

Campaign outcomes are negotiated and often confidential in their details, so the public record is incomplete: a settlement's terms may be disclosed while its side agreements are not, and a withdrawn campaign may reflect a private understanding. Vote counts are published but the beneficial ownership behind them is not. Nothing here recommends a position, and an assessment of a campaign is an assessment of its mechanics and its disclosed evidence, not of its merits.
