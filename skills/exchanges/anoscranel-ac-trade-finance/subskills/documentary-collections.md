# Documentary collections

## Standard

A documentary collection instructs a bank to release documents to the buyer against payment or against acceptance. Unlike a letter of credit, the bank does not undertake to pay; it acts as an intermediary, and the risk of non-payment stays with the seller.

That difference is why collections are cheaper and less protective. The seller ships goods and entrusts the documents to a banking chain that will hand them over when the buyer pays, but if the buyer declines, the goods are already in transit or in a foreign port and the seller bears the cost of retrieving or reselling them. The instrument is appropriate where the parties know each other or where the amount is small.

Primary sources: <https://www.icc-ccs.org/> and <https://www.bis.org/>.

## Decision rules

- Record whether the collection is documents against payment or against acceptance.
- Record the parties and the correspondent banks in the chain.
- Record what happens to the goods if the buyer refuses.
- Record the cost of return or resale in the destination market.
- Record the buyer's credit and history where the relationship is new.
- Record the documents required and whether any are negotiable.

## Procedure

1. Record the collection type and the chain.
2. Record the refusal scenario and its cost.
3. Record the buyer's standing.
4. Record the document set.
5. Compare the cost with a letter of credit.
6. Write the collection summary.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating a collection as equivalent to a letter of credit.
- Ignoring the cost of retrieving refused goods.
- Assuming the bank will withhold documents in a dispute.
- Using a collection for a new counterparty on a large shipment.
- Overlooking a document that is not negotiable when it should be.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Collection type recorded.
- [ ] Chain recorded.
- [ ] Refusal cost estimated.
- [ ] Buyer standing recorded.
- [ ] Document set recorded.
- [ ] Summary written.

The comparison with a letter of credit is what makes the choice legible: a credit costs more and transfers the payment risk to a bank, while a collection costs less and leaves that risk with the seller. For a repeat counterparty with a clean payment history the saving can be rational, and for a first shipment to a new buyer it rarely is, because the cost of a refused collection is the whole cargo rather than the fee difference.
