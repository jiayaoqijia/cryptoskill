# Special and variable dividends

## Standard

The form of a distribution carries information beyond its amount. A regular dividend is a commitment the market capitalises into the share price; a special dividend is explicitly one-off and should not be capitalised; and a variable policy — paying a percentage of earnings each period — converts the distribution into a pass-through rather than a promise.

The choice between forms is a statement about the durability of the cash flow. A company that pays a special rather than raising the regular dividend is saying it does not expect the excess to persist, which is a more honest signal than a raise it may later have to cut. A company that switches from regular to variable is telling the market to stop valuing the payment as an annuity, and the price reaction to that switch is usually about the multiple rather than the cash.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://www.cfainstitute.org/>.

## Decision rules

- Record the form: regular, special, variable, or a combination.
- Record the amount and the period it relates to.
- Record whether the company described it as recurring or one-off.
- Record the funding source: operating cash flow, asset sale, or balance sheet.
- Record any change of form and the market's reaction to it.
- Record the implied annualised yield under each interpretation.

## Procedure

1. Record the declaration and its stated form.
2. Record the funding source where disclosed.
3. Compute the yield under one-off and recurring interpretations.
4. Record any change of form from previous periods.
5. Record the price reaction around the announcement.
6. Write the form analysis.

## Anti-patterns

- Annualising a special dividend into a yield.
- Treating a variable policy as a fixed commitment.
- Ignoring the funding source of a large distribution.
- Missing a change of form, which changes the valuation multiple.
- Comparing yields across companies with different distribution forms.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Form and amount recorded.
- [ ] Funding source recorded.
- [ ] Yields computed under both interpretations.
- [ ] Form changes recorded.
- [ ] Price reaction recorded.
- [ ] Analysis written.

A special dividend also has a mechanical effect that is easy to miss: it lowers the share price by the amount paid on the ex-date, so a holder who measures performance from the cum-date price without adding back the distribution records a loss that did not occur. The record should state the total-return basis it uses whenever a special is in the period.
