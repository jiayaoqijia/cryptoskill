# Plain language

## Standard

- **ISO 24495-1:2023, *Plain language — Part 1: Governing principles and guidelines*.** Four governing principles: readers get what they need (**relevant**), can easily **find** it, can easily **understand** it, and can easily **use** it. 18 guidelines sit under them: identify the readers, their purpose and context; structure for readers; headings that predict what comes next; choose familiar words; write clear, concise sentences and paragraphs; project a respectful tone; ensure cohesion; evaluate with readers (https://www.iso.org/standard/78907.html ⚠️ paywalled; clause titles from previews).
 ISO 24495-2:2025 extends the family to legal communication.
- **The operative guidance, free and current.** digital.gov's plain-language guide runs four sections: principles, writing, design and testing for understanding (https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/). It is the successor home of plainlanguage.gov, which now redirects there.
 Its first rule: "write for your audience." Jargon is "complicated language used to impress, rather than to inform".
 Where no alternative exists, define the term — and do not define common words.
- **The US statutory hook, and its shape.** The Plain Writing Act of 2010 defines plain writing as "clear, concise, well-organized" writing for the field and audience. It requires agencies to use it in covered documents. Its §6 removes judicial review: a governance instrument, not a compliance gate (Pub. L. 111-274, https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-111publ274/html/PLAW-111publ274.htm).
- **Over-simplification has a measured cost.** Simpler text helped low-familiarity readers most, but high-familiarity readers skipped over-simplified summaries — including study limitations (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613904.3642289).

## Decision rules

1. Run the four principles in order: relevant, findable, understandable, usable. A document can fail any one.
2. Choose familiar words. Where no plain word exists, define the term once, then reuse the plain word.
3. Short sentences (GOV.UK splits anything over 25 words), one idea each, paragraphs of at most five sentences.
4. Headings must predict what comes next. "Other information" fails; "How to appeal" passes.
5. Keep supplementary material separate. Do not bury exceptions in the main path.
6. Respect the reader's expertise: expert text keeps its terms and gains clarity from structure.
7. Judge the result with readers, not with a score (subskills/readability-measures.md).

## Procedure

1. Name the reader and their reading context, from the brief (out of scope for this leaf: writing the brief itself).
2. Check relevance: remove every paragraph that does not serve the reader's purpose.
3. Check findability: headings, one idea per section, tables for scan-compare content.
4. Rewrite for understanding: familiar words, short sentences, active voice, defined terms.
5. Rewrite for usability: the reader's task step is visible and ordered.
6. Keep tone respectful. Do not talk down, and do not perform cheerfulness.
7. Evaluate with readers: task-based test with the actual audience, then revise.

## Anti-patterns

- Simplifying an expert document until the precision is gone.
- Defining words the reader already knows.
- Burying exceptions in the main path.
- Headings that label the topic instead of predicting content.
- A rewrite aimed at a score rather than a reader.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Four principles checked in order.
- [ ] Terms defined once, common words left alone.
- [ ] No sentence over 25 words without reason; paragraphs within five sentences.
- [ ] Headings predict their content.
- [ ] Expert content kept precise.
- [ ] Reader evaluation run and recorded.
