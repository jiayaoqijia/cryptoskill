# Shareholder proposals

## Standard

A shareholder proposal is a non-binding request, and its passage does not oblige the company to act. Its value is as a signal: a proposal that receives a majority is a statement about shareholder preferences that the board must respond to, and a proposal that receives a large minority is a warning that the issue will return. The threshold that matters is not fifty percent but the level at which a board changes its position.

The say-on-pay vote is the highest-profile example. It is advisory, it passes almost always, and yet a company that receives a thirty percent vote against is treated as having failed. The interpretation therefore depends on the baseline: what the company's own history is, what the peer group received, and what the company did in response to the last vote.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://www.cfainstitute.org/>.

## Decision rules

- Record the proposal type, its sponsor, and whether it is binding.
- Record the vote result and the threshold applied.
- Record the company's prior results on the same or similar proposals.
- Record the peer group's results for comparability.
- Record the board's stated response to any significant against vote.
- Record whether the proposal can be resubmitted and any exclusion decision.

## Procedure

1. Record the proposal and its sponsor.
2. Record the vote result and threshold.
3. Compare with the company's own history.
4. Compare with the peer group.
5. Record the board's response.
6. Write the interpretation with the baseline stated.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating an advisory vote result as a mandate.
- Reading a high approval as consensus when turnout was low.
- Ignoring the company's own history when the result looks normal.
- Missing a resubmission that shows persistence.
- Treating an exclusion decision as a settled outcome.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Proposal and sponsor recorded.
- [ ] Result and threshold recorded.
- [ ] Company history compared.
- [ ] Peer comparison made.
- [ ] Board response recorded.
- [ ] Interpretation written with baseline.

The threshold that matters is company-specific rather than universal: boards have changed policy after votes as low as twenty percent and ignored votes above fifty, and the difference usually turns on whether the issue is seen as legitimate and whether the same proposal has returned. Reading the result against the company's own responses to past votes is more informative than reading it against an absolute level.
