# Titling and transfer

## Standard

An asset's title decides how it passes, and different titles carry different consequences while the owner is alive: a joint account with right of survivorship passes automatically, but it also gives the other party an interest that can be reached by their creditors and that may upset an intended split. Transfer-on-death provisions pass assets without probate but leave the owner in control until death.

The record lists each asset with its title and the consequence, so that the transfer plan is the sum of the titles rather than an intention layered on top of them. Where the title does not match the intent, that is the finding.

Primary sources: <https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing> and <https://www.irs.gov/>.

## Decision rules

- Every asset is listed with its exact title.
- The transfer consequence of each title is recorded.
- Joint titles are assessed for creditor exposure and for their effect on the intended split.
- Transfer-on-death and payable-on-death provisions are recorded where available and used.
- Titles that do not match the intent are flagged, with the change required.
- Changes to title are recorded with dates and confirmations.

## Procedure

1. List assets with their exact titles.
2. Record the transfer consequence of each.
3. Assess joint titles for exposure and for split effects.
4. Record transfer-on-death options not currently used.
5. Flag mismatches between title and intent.
6. Record the changes made and confirmations received.

## Anti-patterns

- Assuming joint ownership is always the simplest answer.
- Ignoring creditor exposure on a joint asset.
- Adding a child to a title to avoid probate.
- Missing transfer-on-death provisions where offered.
- Relying on intent rather than the recorded title.

- [ ] Assets listed with exact titles.
- [ ] Transfer consequences recorded.
- [ ] Joint titles assessed.

The most common error is adding a person to a title for convenience: it changes ownership, exposes the asset to their circumstances, and often contradicts the split the owner intended. Recording the consequence beside the title makes that trade-off visible before the signature.

Where title and intent diverge, the record should state the divergence plainly and the change that would resolve it, because a title is a legal fact and an intention is not.
