# Campaign economics

## Standard

A campaign is an investment with a budget. The costs are legal, solicitation, public relations, and the cost of the position's capital while it is held; the benefit is the change in the share price, or the settlement that captures it, over the holding period. A campaign that wins a board seat but requires three years to realise value has a different return from one that settles in six months.

The arithmetic also constrains behaviour. A sponsor with a fund approaching the end of its life needs a faster outcome, which is one reason campaigns against companies with long resolution cycles are rarer. Recorded costs and a stated holding period convert a public-relations narrative into a return calculation, and the calculation is what makes a settlement acceptable or not.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://www.cfainstitute.org/>.

## Decision rules

- Record the position size and its cost basis.
- Record the disclosed campaign expenses where available.
- Record the stated holding period and the sponsor's fund horizon.
- Record the price change since the filing and the change at settlement or vote.
- Compute the return over the holding period and annualise it.
- Record the return the sponsor requires, if disclosed or inferable.

## Procedure

1. Record the position and its cost.
2. Record campaign expenses where disclosed.
3. Record the holding period and fund horizon.
4. Record the price change from disclosure to resolution.
5. Compute and annualise the return.
6. Write the economics with the assumptions stated.

## Anti-patterns

- Judging a campaign by its outcome without the time taken to reach it.
- Ignoring the cost of carrying the position.
- Treating a settlement as a win regardless of the price achieved.
- Assuming the disclosed expenses are complete.
- Ignoring the sponsor's fund horizon as a constraint.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Position and cost recorded.
- [ ] Expenses recorded where disclosed.
- [ ] Holding period and horizon recorded.
- [ ] Price change recorded.
- [ ] Return computed and annualised.
- [ ] Assumptions stated.

The cost side also includes the position's own risk: a campaign typically involves a concentrated stake in a single company, so the sponsor's return is exposed to idiosyncratic outcomes that a diversified holder does not bear, and the return required to justify that concentration is higher than the return required to justify the same position inside a portfolio. That difference is what makes some campaigns rational for the sponsor and unattractive for a follower.
