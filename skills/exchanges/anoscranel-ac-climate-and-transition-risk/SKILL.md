# Climate and transition risk

## Overview

Climate risk enters a valuation through two doors. Transition risk is the cost of moving to a lower-carbon economy: carbon prices, regulation, technology substitution, and the write-down of assets that become uneconomic. Physical risk is the cost of a changing climate: floods, heat, drought, and the insurance and capital costs that follow. Both are measurable in principle and both are frequently reduced to a score that measures neither.

This skill covers the measurement: what the emissions scopes actually count, what the disclosure regimes require and where they leave gaps, what a climate scenario can and cannot tell you, how transition and physical risk show up by sector and by location, and the arithmetic of an asset whose value depends on a carbon price that does not yet exist.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://www.bis.org/>.

## When to use

- When a company's climate disclosure is being read or questioned.
- When transition or physical risk needs to enter a valuation.
- When a carbon price or a stranded-asset question arises.
- When comparing disclosures across companies or regimes.

## When NOT to use

- For ESG scores and ratings as products (use ac-equity-research).
- For commodity markets including carbon allowances as tradable instruments (use ac-commodities-trading).
- For real-asset and infrastructure valuation (use ac-alternatives-and-real-assets).

## Status and honest limits

Emissions data are estimated more often than measured, reported annually with a lag, and inconsistent across regimes; scope 3 in particular is a modelled figure with wide error bands. Scenarios are conditional forecasts, not probabilities, and using one as an expectation is a misreading. Nothing here recommends a security or predicts policy, and a climate adjustment to a valuation is only as good as the emissions figure and the scenario assumption behind it.
