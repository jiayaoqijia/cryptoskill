# Hedging programme design

## Standard

A hedging programme answers four questions and is only coherent when all four are stated: which exposures are hedged, what proportion, over what horizon, and with which instruments. A company that hedges its currency exposure for one year but borrows at floating rates for five has a policy with an unstated boundary, and a company that hedges ninety percent of a forecast that turns out wrong has created a loss rather than a protection.

The proportion is the decision that most often goes unexamined. Hedging is not free — it has a cost in foregone upside and in transaction costs — and the right proportion depends on the certainty of the exposure, the sensitivity of the business's margins, and how much variability the business can absorb. A hedge ratio chosen because it is conventional rather than because it matches those factors is a ratio that will be changed in the first adverse move.

Primary sources: <https://www.fasb.org/> and <https://www.bis.org/>.

## Decision rules

- Record each exposure type: currency, interest rate, commodity, or another.
- Record the proportion hedged and the basis for it.
- Record the horizon and whether it matches the exposure's own duration.
- Record the instruments used and their cost.
- Record the sensitivity disclosed, which shows how much remains unhedged.
- Record whether the programme hedges a transaction exposure, a forecast, or a translation exposure, since each behaves differently.

## Procedure

1. Read the risk management disclosure for the programme's scope.
2. Record the exposure types and their approximate size.
3. Record the hedge proportion and horizon per exposure.
4. Record the instruments and their cost.
5. Record the disclosed sensitivity of results to a move.
6. Write the assessment with the unhedged remainder named.

## Anti-patterns

- Judging a programme by the instruments rather than by the proportion and horizon.
- Assuming a hedge reduces risk when it hedges a forecast that may not occur.
- Ignoring the mismatch between a one-year hedge and five-year debt.
- Treating a translation hedge as protection for cash flow.
- Overlooking the cost of the hedge in the business's margin.
