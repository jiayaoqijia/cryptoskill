# General obligation versus revenue

## Standard

The distinction is what the bondholder can reach. A general obligation bond is backed by the issuer's full faith and credit, which in practice means its taxing power and its ability to raise revenue across the whole jurisdiction. A revenue bond is backed only by the cash flow of a named project — a toll road, a utility, a hospital — and the holder has no claim on the issuer's general resources if that project's revenue falls short.

The difference shows up in the recovery, not in the coupon. A revenue bond on a project with a structural demand shortfall can default while the city behind it is fiscally sound, and the bondholder's remedy is limited to the project and whatever reserve fund stands behind it. Reading the pledge means asking two questions: which cash flow pays the debt, and what happens when that cash flow is insufficient.

Primary sources: <https://www.msrb.org/> and <https://www.sec.gov/investor>.

## Decision rules

- Record the pledge type: general obligation, revenue, or a hybrid such as a moral obligation or an appropriation pledge.
- Record the specific revenue source for a revenue bond, and whether it is essential-service or discretionary.
- Record the rate covenant and the additional-bonds test, which govern future debt against the same revenue.
- Record the debt service reserve fund and its size in months of debt service.
- Record what the holder can reach if revenue falls short of the covenant.
- Record any state credit enhancement or intercept mechanism.

## Procedure

1. Read the official statement's security and sources-of-payment section.
2. Classify the pledge and name the revenue source.
3. Record the rate covenant and additional-bonds test.
4. Record the reserve fund and any enhancement.
5. Record the remedy on a shortfall.
6. Write the credit summary with the pledge stated plainly.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating a revenue bond as backed by the sponsoring government.
- Reading the rate covenant without checking whether rates have been raised as required.
- Ignoring the additional-bonds test, which lets leverage rise against the same revenue.
- Assuming a reserve fund covers a prolonged shortfall.
- Confusing an appropriation pledge with a full faith and credit obligation.
