# Equity research

## Overview

Equity research answers one question before valuation is attempted: what kind of business is this, and can I trust its numbers? The work is reading primary documents, checking that the three statements agree with each other, testing the competitive story against evidence, and writing down what is known, what is inferred, and what is unknown.

The discipline that separates research from opinion is sourcing. A claim about revenue growth is a filing line; a claim about a moat is an inference that needs evidence; a claim about management quality is a judgement that needs a record. This skill keeps those three in separate columns.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/> and <https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing>.

## When to use

- Before any first purchase of a listed company's shares.
- When a held company reports results, changes auditor, or raises capital.
- When a thesis rests on a story that has not been checked against filings.
- Before writing the research record that valuation will build on.

## When NOT to use

- For estimating what the business is worth (use ac-valuation).
- For deciding allocation across holdings (use ac-investment-policy-statement).
- For evaluating a crypto token's supply and governance (use ac-token-diligence).

## Status and honest limits

Public filings describe the past and are prepared by the company being studied. Ratios are only as comparable as the accounting behind them, and a moat is a hypothesis until it has survived a competitive attack. The record must therefore carry confidence levels and an explicit unknowns section; a research note that reads as uniformly certain is not research, it is advocacy.
