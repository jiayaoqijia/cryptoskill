# Capital return policy

## Overview

A company that generates more cash than it can reinvest has to decide what to do with it: hold it, pay it out as a dividend, buy back its own shares, or repay debt. That decision is a policy, not an event, and its quality shows over a cycle rather than in a single quarter. The mechanics matter because the same dollar of capital returned can be returned well or badly.

This skill covers those mechanics and the arithmetic behind them: how buybacks are executed and what execution quality looks like, how dividend policy is set and what makes it sustainable, the difference between regular, special and variable distributions, the comparison of shareholder yield against other uses of cash, and the leverage question when buybacks are funded with debt.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://www.cfainstitute.org/>.

## When to use

- When analysing a company's buyback programme and how it is executed.
- When assessing whether a dividend is sustainable.
- When comparing capital return with debt reduction or reinvestment.
- When a company announces a special dividend, an ASR, or a policy change.

## When NOT to use

- For scoring management's overall capital allocation record (use ac-equity-research).
- For the tax treatment of distributions (use ac-tax-aware-investing).
- For price and volume adjustment around corporate actions (use ac-corporate-actions).

## Status and honest limits

Buyback and dividend data are disclosed with delays and with gaps: programmes authorise amounts without committing to use them, execution is reported quarterly at best, and the price paid is often not disclosed. A conclusion about execution quality is therefore an inference from what is visible rather than a measurement of what happened. Nothing here recommends a security, and a buyback announcement is an authorisation rather than a return of capital until the shares are actually purchased.
