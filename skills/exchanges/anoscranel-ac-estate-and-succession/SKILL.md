# Estate and succession

## Overview

A portfolio outlives its owner, and the transfer is governed by documents most investors never read: beneficiary designations on accounts, titling on joint assets, and the will that governs everything the designations do not. The first principle is that designations usually override the will, which means an outdated form can defeat a carefully drafted plan.

The second principle is liquidity. Estates have costs — taxes, fees, debts — and a portfolio of illiquid assets may force a sale at the worst moment. The craft is preparing the record: what is held, how it is titled, who receives it, and what an executor would need to act.

Primary sources: <https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing> and <https://www.irs.gov/>.

## When to use

- When reviewing beneficiary designations on accounts and policies.
- When titling of assets is being decided or changed.
- When preparing the information an executor would need.
- When a family change makes the current arrangements stale.

## When NOT to use

- For drafting a will, trust, or power of attorney: consult a qualified professional in the jurisdiction.
- For the tax computation on transfers (use ac-trading-records-and-tax).
- For insurance coverage decisions (use ac-insurance-and-risk-transfer).

## Status and honest limits

Succession law is jurisdictional and changes; this skill does not interpret it. Every leaf therefore ends at the boundary of preparation, and the record names the questions left for a professional. A plan that is not reviewed after a family change, an account change, or a move to another jurisdiction is a plan that may transfer the wrong assets to the wrong people.
