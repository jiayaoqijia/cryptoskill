# Drafting

## Standard

- **Revision is where the volume is not, and where the argument moves.** Author self-revision is light and local. Median sentence update ratio is 19.0% across 751 aligned arXiv papers, with 3.7% almost completely rewritten. Peer review reaches much further into what is claimed (<https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.15067>). 60.2% of primary claims are touched between preprint and publication across 72,644 abstract pairs. Keep the units apart; neither number sizes the other.
- **What a revision pass buys.** Technical editing between acceptance and publication improves readability only slightly. It improves reference accuracy and abstract quality (JAMA 2002; 285:2215, https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/194990). The systematic review's own verdict is that "remarkably little is known" about which processes contribute most.
- **Outlining buys translation time.** Gains appear by letting writers spend composition time on translating rather than planning (Kellogg 1988). They attach to inexperienced writers, not experienced ones writing routine documents.
- **Pass placement.** Compress, tighten and language polish are automatable. Outline and argument architecture, the contribution claim, limitations, and what the data does not show are the human seat.

## Decision rules

1. A full rough draft beats a perfect paragraph. Completeness first, quality second.
2. Write the sections you understand best first. The hard opening comes last.
3. Never polish a sentence while the draft is unfinished. Marking a problem is allowed; fixing it is not.
4. Unknown facts get a placeholder tag and a note, not a pause in the writing.
5. Blocked means the outline or the brief is wrong, or the research is missing. Fix that, not the prose.
6. Draft in one sitting where possible. A draft assembled in fragments drifts in register.

## Procedure

1. Open the brief and the outline side by side. Confirm the one-sentence answer is on screen.
2. Set a target length from the venue rules. Count words, not pages.
3. Write section by section, in this order: the sections you know, then the hard opening, then the summary.
4. Tag gaps inline as `[TK: what is missing]` and continue. Never stop to research mid-paragraph.
5. Write the summary last, from the finished body — never from the plan.
6. Leave the draft for a delay before any revision. Delay beats re-reading immediately (out of scope for this leaf: the structural-revision passes).
7. Run the placeholder list: every `[TK]` becomes a research task or a cut section.

## Anti-patterns

- Paragraph one rewritten eleven times while the document has no end.
- Stopping to verify a number mid-paragraph, then losing the thread.
- Drafting from the plan's wording instead of the plan's structure.
- A draft assembled from fragments written on different days, in different registers.
- Summary written first and never reconciled with the body.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Every section drafted, including the hard ones.
- [ ] All gaps tagged as `[TK]`, none left unmarked.
- [ ] Target length recorded against venue rules.
- [ ] Summary written last, from the body.
- [ ] Draft left to rest before revision.
- [ ] Placeholder list handed to the research step.
