# Portfolio financing

## Overview

A private fund's reported performance and an investor's ability to exit are both affected by financing arrangements that sit outside the assets themselves. A subscription line lets the fund delay calling capital, which changes the timing of cash flows and therefore the internal rate of return. A NAV loan borrows against the fund's portfolio, adding leverage at the fund level. A continuation fund moves assets from an expiring vehicle into a new one, giving some investors liquidity and others a decision.

Each of these is a legitimate tool and each creates a conflict, because the party arranging the financing or the continuation is the same party that reports the performance and decides the terms. This skill covers the mechanics, the arithmetic that makes them legible, and the conflicts that an investor has to price.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://www.federalreserve.gov/>.

## When to use

- When a fund's reported return relies on subscription-line financing.
- When an LP is offered a rollover into a continuation vehicle.
- When evaluating an LP-led secondary and its discount.
- When fund-level leverage or a NAV facility appears in the documents.

## When NOT to use

- For manager operations, fees, and terms (use ac-manager-and-vehicle-diligence).
- For private credit as an asset class (use ac-private-credit).
- For the allocation decision across asset classes (use ac-alternatives-and-real-assets).

## Status and honest limits

These arrangements are documented in agreements that investors often receive late and with limited negotiation. Reported performance figures rarely restate the effect of a subscription line, and a continuation's fairness opinion is commissioned by the sponsor. Nothing here recommends a fund or a transaction, and a return comparison that has not adjusted for the financing structure is a comparison between two different businesses.
