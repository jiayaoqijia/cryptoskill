# Recurring one-offs

## Standard

A restructuring charge is described as one-off because it relates to a specific action, and the description is accurate for the action even when the charges recur annually. A company that restructures in four consecutive years has a recurring operating cost, and the label tells you about the presentation rather than about the economics. The same applies to impairments, which often follow a period of accrual build and mark the moment the estimates were revised.

The test is arithmetic and simple: add the charges back over the window and ask what the profit would have been without the exclusion. If the cumulative adjustment is small relative to profit, the label is defensible; if it is large and repeated, the adjusted figure describes a business the company does not operate. The record should present both series side by side.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/> and <https://www.sec.gov/investor>.

## Decision rules

- Record each restructuring and impairment charge by period, with its stated purpose.
- Count the consecutive periods containing such charges.
- Compute the cumulative charge across the window and its share of reported profit.
- Record whether the charge is cash or non-cash.
- Record any subsequent reversal and the period it appeared in.
- Record what the profit would have been with the charges included in each period.

## Procedure

1. List the charges by period with their stated purposes.
2. Count the periods in which they appear.
3. Compute the cumulative total and its share of profit.
4. Classify each as cash or non-cash.
5. Track reversals.
6. Write the comparison of reported and unadjusted profit.

## Anti-patterns

- Accepting the one-off label without counting the periods.
- Ignoring the cash portion of a restructuring charge.
- Treating an impairment as unrelated to prior growth.
- Missing a reversal that flatters a later period.
- Comparing adjusted profit across companies with different charge patterns.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Charges listed by period.
- [ ] Consecutive periods counted.
- [ ] Cumulative share computed.
- [ ] Cash versus non-cash classified.
- [ ] Reversals tracked.
- [ ] Unadjusted comparison written.

The reversal is the detail most easily missed: an impairment recognised in a weak year reduces future depreciation, which raises later profit without any operational improvement, and a company that combines a large impairment with a subsequent earnings recovery should have that sequence noted explicitly rather than credited to the recovery.
