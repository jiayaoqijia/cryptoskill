# Claims and records

## Standard

A claim is decided on evidence, and the evidence is created before the loss. Photographs of the property, an inventory of the contents, receipts, the policy documents, and a record of the conversations with the broker: each is trivial to create while nothing has happened and impossible to reconstruct afterwards.

The record also documents the coverage itself: what is insured, at what limit, with which deductible, and when it was last reviewed. That record is what turns a claim conversation from a memory exercise into a checklist.

Primary sources: <https://www.naic.org/> and <https://www.usa.gov/>.

## Decision rules

- Every coverage has its policy number, limit, deductible, and renewal date recorded in one place.
- Property and contents evidence is dated and stored off-site.
- Conversations with brokers and insurers are recorded with dates and outcomes.
- The claims process is written down: who to call, what to submit, and in what order.
- Evidence is refreshed at each renewal.
- The record is accessible to someone who has to act for the household.

## Procedure

1. Build the coverage register: policy, entity, limit, deductible, renewal.
2. Photograph and inventory the insured property; store the evidence off-site.
3. Record the broker and insurer contacts with the policy.
4. Write the claims process and the documents each step needs.
5. Refresh the evidence at each renewal.
6. Test the register by answering a hypothetical claim from it.

## Anti-patterns

- Holding the only copy of the policy at home.
- No inventory of contents.
- Relying on memory for a conversation with the insurer.
- Not knowing which entity underwrites the policy.
- Refreshing the evidence only after a claim.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Coverage register complete with limits and renewals.
- [ ] Property evidence dated and stored off-site.
- [ ] Contacts recorded.
- [ ] Claims process written with required documents.
- [ ] Evidence refreshed at renewal.
- [ ] Register tested against a hypothetical claim.

## Sources

- State insurance consumer guidance: <https://www.naic.org/>

The register's test is a stranger's test: someone who has never seen the household should be able to read it and know which policy covers which risk, whom to call, and where the evidence sits. If the register needs explaining, the claim will need explaining too, and the explaining happens at the worst possible moment.
