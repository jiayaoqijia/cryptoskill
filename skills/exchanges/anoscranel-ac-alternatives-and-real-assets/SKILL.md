# Alternatives and real assets

## Overview

Alternatives are sold on properties that public markets are said to lack: diversification, inflation protection, income, and a premium for accepting illiquidity. Some of those claims are well supported, some are conditional, and some are accounting artefacts of how the assets are valued. The work is to tell them apart before capital is committed, because the exit from an alternative is usually slower and more expensive than the entry.

Every asset class in this skill shares one structural feature: the price you see is either a market price with thin trading behind it, or an appraisal that nobody has tested with a sale. That single fact drives most of the discipline here.

Primary sources: <https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing> and <https://www.sec.gov/investor>.

## When to use

- Before any first exposure to real estate, commodities, collectibles, or private vehicles.
- When an alternatives allocation is being justified by a diversification or inflation claim.
- When a private asset's valuation or liquidity terms are being assessed.
- When a portfolio review touches an illiquid holding.

## When NOT to use

- For public equity or bond analysis (use ac-equity-research, ac-fixed-income-and-rates).
- For the portfolio's allocation policy and constraints (use ac-investment-policy-statement).
- For the valuation of an operating business (use ac-valuation).

## Status and honest limits

Illiquid assets are valued by methods that assume calm markets, and their reported volatility is therefore understated — which makes diversification statistics look better than the risk. The honest posture is to treat every alternative's valuation as a claim, its liquidity terms as a constraint, and its role in the portfolio as an empirical question rather than a marketing premise.
