# Positioning record

## Standard

The positioning record states the claim, the disclosure it rests on, the date of that disclosure, the lag to today, and the coverage limit of the source. Those five fields are what separate an evidence-based statement about institutions from a story about them, and they are the fields most often omitted when positioning is cited in a discussion.

The record also states what would change the conclusion. Because every source here is stale and partial, a conclusion built on one is provisional by construction, and the trigger that would overturn it — a new filing, a reversal in the fails series, a change in the reported category — should be named when the conclusion is written rather than discovered later.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://osf.io/prereg/>.

## Decision rules

- The record states the claim in one sentence.
- Every source is named with its date, its publication date, and its coverage limit.
- The lag between the source date and the claim date is stated in days.
- The record distinguishes disclosed position from inferred intent.
- The falsifier is named: which new filing or data point would change the conclusion.
- The record is dated and versioned; later filings produce amendments, not rewrites.

## Procedure

1. Write the claim and the question it answers.
2. List each source with its dates and coverage.
3. Compute the lag.
4. Separate disclosed fact from inference.
5. Name the falsifier.
6. File and date the record.

## Anti-patterns

- Citing "institutional interest" without a filing.
- Mixing disclosures of different dates into one statement.
- Presenting inference as disclosure.
- No falsifier, so the conclusion can never be reviewed.
- Rewriting the record after a filing contradicts it.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Claim stated in one sentence.
- [ ] Sources named with dates and coverage.
- [ ] Lag computed.
- [ ] Fact and inference separated.
- [ ] Falsifier named.
- [ ] Record dated and filed.

The record also benefits from a standing question: which disclosure would I need to see to believe the opposite. If no filing could answer that, the conclusion is not evidence-based but narrative, and the honest record says so rather than dressing an impression in the vocabulary of filings.


Keeping the record in one place per security, with amendments appended rather than rewritten, is what turns a series of one-off observations into a history that can be read for change; a folder of separate notes cannot answer whether institutional holding has been rising for four quarters.
