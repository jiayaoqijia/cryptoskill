# Direct real estate

## Overview

A direct property is valued on the income it produces, and that income depends on contracts, tenants, and physical condition. The arithmetic is simple — net operating income divided by a capitalisation rate gives a value — and every input to it is a judgement: the income assumes leases renew at stated terms, the expenses assume a maintenance level, and the rate assumes a buyer would accept that yield for that risk.

This skill covers those judgements and the documents behind them: how net operating income is built and where it can be flattered, how lease terms and tenant credit determine stability, how capitalisation rates relate to financing and growth, how appraisals differ from market prices, and how property-level debt and deferred capital expenditure change the equity return. It analyses the income; it does not select a property.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://www.federalreserve.gov/>.

## When to use

- When evaluating a direct property or a single-asset deal.
- When net operating income, a capitalisation rate, or an appraisal needs testing.
- When lease terms, tenant concentration, or rollover risk need assessing.
- When property-level debt and capital expenditure affect the equity case.

## When NOT to use

- For real-estate funds and vehicles (use ac-alternatives-and-real-assets).
- For listed REIT equity analysis (use ac-equity-research).
- For mortgage-backed securities and their waterfalls (use ac-securitisation-and-tranches).

## Status and honest limits

Property valuations are appraisals, and an appraisal is an opinion formed at a date on stated assumptions. Illiquid assets are marked infrequently, which means a reported value can lag the market by quarters in either direction. Nothing here recommends a property, and a valuation that has not been tested against its own assumptions and against transaction evidence is a number rather than a measurement.
