# Sovereign debt

## Overview

A sovereign borrower cannot be liquidated, and that single fact organises everything about its debt. There is no collateral to seize, no court that can order payment against its will, and no process that converts claims into equity. What exists instead is a negotiation in which the creditor's leverage is the cost of exclusion from future market access, and the debtor's leverage is the willingness to impose losses.

This skill covers the machinery of that negotiation: how collective action clauses let a majority bind a minority, how the restructuring proceeds from exchange to programme, why the law governing the bond decides which creditors can be held out, how recovery is estimated, and what the exit yield signals. Macro reading and currency risk belong to other skills; this one is about the instruments and the process.

Primary sources: <https://www.imf.org/> and <https://www.bis.org/>.

## When to use

- When analysing sovereign debt and the possibility of restructuring.
- When a collective action or aggregation clause determines who is bound.
- When comparing local-law and foreign-law instruments of the same issuer.
- When estimating recovery or reading an exit yield.

## When NOT to use

- For reading macroeconomic releases and policy signals (use ac-macro-and-policy-reading).
- For corporate credit analysis (use ac-bank-and-credit-analysis).
- For currency exposure and hedging in a portfolio (use ac-currency-and-fx-risk).

## Status and honest limits

Sovereign restructurings are negotiated outcomes shaped by politics, law, and the debtor's willingness to sustain losses, none of which is forecastable from the instruments. The clauses described here are contractual and their drafting varies, so the document rather than the template is authoritative. Nothing here recommends a bond, and a recovery estimate for a sovereign is an assumption about a negotiation rather than a calculation about a business.
