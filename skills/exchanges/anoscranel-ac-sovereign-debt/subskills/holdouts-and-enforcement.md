# Holdouts and enforcement

## Standard

A holdout buys a distressed sovereign bond at a discount and refuses the restructuring offer, seeking either full payment or a settlement on better terms. The strategy worked historically because a creditor could sue in a foreign court, obtain a judgment, and pursue the sovereign's commercial assets abroad, and because the threat of that pursuit gave the holdout leverage in the debtor's return to market.

The constraints have tightened. Collective action clauses with aggregation bind a larger minority; the official sector has adopted policies that limit its willingness to lend to a debtor that has not treated private creditors comparably; and the enforcement route depends on finding assets that are both reachable and not protected by sovereign immunity. A holdout is now a legal position with a settlement value rather than a route to full recovery.

Primary sources: <https://www.imf.org/> and <https://www.bis.org/>.

## Decision rules

- Record the instrument, its governing law, and whether a collective action clause binds the position.
- Record the price paid and the claim's face value.
- Record the enforcement route available and the immunity protections that apply.
- Record the assets the sovereign holds in the enforcing jurisdiction.
- Record the official sector's comparability policy and how it affects the debtor's incentive.
- Record the settlement history for comparable holdout positions.

## Procedure

1. Record the instrument, law, and clause position.
2. Record the price and face value.
3. Record the enforcement route and immunity limits.
4. Record reachable assets.
5. Record the official policy context.
6. Write the assessment with the settlement value estimated.

## Anti-patterns

- Assuming a historical holdout result is available today.
- Ignoring the collective action clause that binds the position.
- Overlooking immunity protections on central-bank and diplomatic assets.
- Treating a judgment as equivalent to a recovery.
- Ignoring the official sector's comparability stance.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Instrument and law recorded.
- [ ] Clause position recorded.
- [ ] Enforcement route recorded.
- [ ] Reachable assets recorded.
- [ ] Official policy recorded.
- [ ] Settlement value estimated.

The economics also depend on the debtor's cost of staying in default: a sovereign with no near-term financing need can wait a holdout out for years, while one that needs market access must settle. That asymmetry, rather than the strength of the legal claim, usually sets the settlement value, which is why the record should state the debtor's financing position alongside the enforcement route.
