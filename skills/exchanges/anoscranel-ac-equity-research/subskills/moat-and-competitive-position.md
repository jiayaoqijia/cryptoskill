# Moat and competitive position

## Standard

A moat is a claim about the future: that a company can keep earning above its cost of capital because something makes it hard to compete away. The four mechanisms that can produce that are switching costs, network effects, cost advantage, and intangible assets such as brands or patents. Each has a test, and the test is evidence, not adjectives in an annual report.

The common failure is accepting a moat story that the numbers do not support. If a company claims pricing power, margins should be stable or rising through a cycle. If it claims switching costs, customer retention should be high and churn low. A moat that never shows up in a financial series is a narrative.

Primary sources: <https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/> and <https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/>.

## Decision rules

- Name the mechanism explicitly: switching costs, network effects, cost advantage, or intangibles. "Strong brand" alone is not a mechanism.
- For each mechanism, state the observable that would confirm it and the one that would refute it.
- Pricing power is tested against margin history through at least one downturn.
- Retention and churn are read from disclosure, not inferred from satisfaction language.
- A moat claim that survives five years of competition is evidence; one that has never been attacked is a hypothesis.
- Record the strongest counter-argument you can construct.

## Procedure

1. Extract the company's own moat claims from the annual report.
2. Map each claim to one of the four mechanisms.
3. For each, gather the confirming and refuting observables from filings.
4. Check margin and return history through a downturn where one exists.
5. Write the strongest counter-argument (how a competitor could attack).
6. Record the verdict per mechanism: evidenced, plausible, or unsupported.

## Anti-patterns

- Accepting "brand strength" as a mechanism without a financial series behind it.
- Confusing a growing market with a moat; growth can hide the absence of one.
- Ignoring a competitor that has already attacked and won share.
- Treating patents as permanent without checking expiry and litigation.
- Skipping the counter-argument because the story is appealing.
