# Forensic record

## Standard

The forensic record states the question, the document and line item that prompted it, the alternative explanations considered, and what would resolve it. That structure keeps the record on the right side of the line between analysis and accusation: a reader can see precisely what was observed, where, and what remains unknown.

The alternative-explanations field is what makes the record credible. Every observation in this skill has a legitimate explanation somewhere, and a record that lists the plausible benign ones and says why they were not sufficient is a record that a reader can trust. One that lists only the concerning reading is advocacy, and advocacy in this domain is both unfair and, in practice, wrong more often than its author expects.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://osf.io/prereg/>.

## Decision rules

- Record the question in one sentence.
- Record the document, period, and line item that raised it.
- Record the plausible alternative explanations and their status.
- Record what evidence would resolve the question.
- Record the conclusion as a status: open, explained, or unresolved.
- Record the date and the source document version.

## Procedure

1. State the question.
2. Cite the document, period, and line.
3. List alternative explanations.
4. State what would resolve it.
5. Assign a status.
6. Date the record and reference the filing.

## Anti-patterns

- Recording an observation without its source line.
- Listing only the concerning explanation.
- Stating a conclusion where the evidence supports a question.
- Leaving a question without a resolution path.
- Failing to note the filing version examined.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Question stated.
- [ ] Source cited precisely.
- [ ] Alternatives listed.
- [ ] Resolution path stated.
- [ ] Status assigned.
- [ ] Date and version recorded.

The record's most valuable section is the one that says what would settle the question, because it converts an open concern into a testable item that a later reader can check against a subsequent filing, which is how a question becomes an explanation rather than remaining an insinuation that nobody resolves.

Keeping the records together across periods also shows which questions have already been answered: a reader who can see that a concern raised two years ago was subsequently explained by a disclosed transaction will not raise it again, which is the practical benefit of writing the status rather than only the observation.
