# Methodology integrity

## Standard

An index's character is its rule set, and a change to the rules changes the index even when the name stays the same. Rule changes happen for legitimate reasons — market structure evolves, data improves — and they also happen in ways that favour the provider's products or the largest constituents. Distinguishing the two requires reading the change and its stated rationale rather than the announcement.

The test is whether the change was consulted on, whether its effect on the index's stated purpose is described, and whether it increases or reduces the concentration and turnover the rules produce. A methodology that repeatedly revises toward the largest constituents is one whose exposure has drifted from its name, and the record should note the drift and its dates.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing>.

## Decision rules

- Record each methodology change with its date and stated rationale.
- Record whether a consultation period preceded it.
- Record the change's effect on concentration and turnover.
- Record the change's effect on the index's stated purpose.
- Record who benefits from the change, where identifiable.
- Record the cumulative effect of changes over several years.

## Procedure

1. Collect the methodology versions and their dates.
2. Record each change and its rationale.
3. Assess the consultation.
4. Measure the effect on concentration and turnover.
5. Assess the cumulative drift.
6. Write the integrity assessment.

## Anti-patterns

- Assuming an index's character is stable because its name is.
- Reading an announcement rather than the rule text.
- Treating a consultation as evidence of good faith without reading the responses.
- Ignoring cumulative drift in favour of individual changes.
- Alleging motive where only the effect is observable.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Changes and dates recorded.
- [ ] Rationale recorded.
- [ ] Consultation recorded.
- [ ] Concentration and turnover effects measured.
- [ ] Cumulative drift assessed.
- [ ] Assessment limited to effects.

The cumulative test is the more informative one: a single rule change can be defensible while a sequence of changes in the same direction describes a drift, and only the version history makes that visible.


A useful discipline is to record only effects and dates, leaving motive to inference: the drift is observable in the data, and the record is stronger when it states what changed rather than why someone might have wanted it.
