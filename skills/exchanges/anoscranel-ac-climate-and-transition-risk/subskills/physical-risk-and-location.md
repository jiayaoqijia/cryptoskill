# Physical risk and location

## Standard

Physical risk is a property of a place, not of a sector. A warehouse in a flood plain, a data centre in a heat-stressed grid, and a farm in a drought region face different hazards, and their exposure depends on the asset's own resilience, on the insurance available, and on the cost of adapting. A sector-level score averages those away.

The financial transmission runs through three channels: damage and business interruption, insurance cost and availability, and the cost of capital as lenders and insurers reprice. The third is often the largest and the slowest, and it is the one most likely to be missed, because a company can absorb a bad year but not a permanent change in what its assets cost to insure and finance.

Primary sources: <https://www.eia.gov/> and <https://www.bis.org/>.

## Decision rules

- Record each material asset's location and the dominant hazards there.
- Record the asset's own resilience and any adaptation already in place.
- Record the insurance arrangements, limits, and recent cost trend.
- Record the dependency on infrastructure that is itself exposed.
- Record the cost of adaptation and who bears it.
- Record the financing exposure: lenders, insurers, and any repricing evidence.

## Procedure

1. Map material assets to locations and hazards.
2. Record resilience and adaptation.
3. Record insurance terms and cost trend.
4. Record infrastructure dependencies.
5. Estimate adaptation cost.
6. Write the assessment by asset rather than by sector.

## Anti-patterns

- Using a country-level score for a specific site.
- Ignoring insurance availability as a transmission channel.
- Assuming adaptation cost is optional.
- Overlooking infrastructure that the asset depends on.
- Treating a single bad season as evidence of a trend.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Assets mapped to hazards.
- [ ] Resilience recorded.
- [ ] Insurance terms recorded.
- [ ] Infrastructure dependencies recorded.
- [ ] Adaptation cost estimated.
- [ ] Assessment written by asset.

The insurance channel deserves a number where possible: a company that discloses its insurance cost trend across several years is disclosing a market's view of its physical risk, and that view is produced by people with the loss data, which makes it more informative than any externally computed hazard score applied from a distance.


The adaptation question also has an ownership dimension: a tenant whose landlord owns the building cannot decide whether to flood-proof it, and a company operating under a lease may bear the interruption while bearing none of the remedy. Recording who controls the asset, not only where it is, is what determines whether the risk can be managed at all.
