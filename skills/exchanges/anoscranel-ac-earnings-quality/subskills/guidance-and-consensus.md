# Guidance and consensus

## Standard

A company's guidance is a range it chooses to publish, and the range is a communication rather than a forecast: its width, its midpoint, and the frequency with which it is revised all carry information. Guidance that is consistently beaten is a guidance policy as much as a forecasting record, and a company that stops giving guidance has changed what the market can measure it against.

Consensus is a different object: it is the average of analysts' estimates, whose composition is unknown unless the provider discloses it. A consensus of three analysts is not the same as one of thirty, and the dispersion matters more than the mean when a result surprises. The record states the number of estimates, the range, and the date the consensus was taken.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/> and <https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing>.

## Decision rules

- Record the guidance range, its midpoint, and the metric it applies to.
- Record the guidance history: how often it was raised, cut, or beaten.
- Record the consensus figure, the number of estimates, and the range.
- Record the date the consensus was collected, since it moves.
- Record any change in the metrics the company guides on.
- Where guidance is withdrawn, record the date and what replaced it.

## Procedure

1. Pull the guidance from the release or the filing.
2. Record its metric, range, and midpoint.
3. Build the guidance history across periods.
4. Record consensus with its composition and date.
5. Compare the dispersion with the expected surprise.
6. Write the expectations summary.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating a guidance midpoint as a forecast.
- Comparing a result with consensus of unknown composition.
- Ignoring the dispersion behind an average.
- Missing a change in the metric being guided.
- Reading a beat as operational strength when guidance was set low.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Guidance range and metric recorded.
- [ ] Guidance history recorded.
- [ ] Consensus figure and range recorded.
- [ ] Composition and date recorded.
- [ ] Metric changes recorded.
- [ ] Expectations summary written.

The comparison that carries information is against the company's own guidance rather than against consensus alone: a company that guides conservatively and beats by a consistent margin is telling the market something about how it manages expectations, and that pattern is a feature of its communication rather than evidence about the business.
