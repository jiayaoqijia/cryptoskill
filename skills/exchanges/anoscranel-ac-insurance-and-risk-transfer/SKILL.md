# Insurance and risk transfer

## Overview

Insurance converts a large, uncertain loss into a small, certain payment. That is worth doing when the loss would be unaffordable and unlikely to be self-funded, and it is not worth doing when the loss is small relative to the premium or frequent enough that the insurer's load dominates. The craft is telling those two cases apart and reading the policy that governs the transfer.

This skill covers the decision and the document. It does not sell, recommend, or price coverage, and it does not interpret a policy for a claim; those need a broker or an adviser with the actual wording. What it does is make the record explicit: which risks are retained, which are transferred, at what limits, and at what cost.

Primary sources: <https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing> and <https://www.naic.org/>.

## When to use

- When deciding whether a risk should be retained or transferred.
- When reviewing coverage limits, deductibles, or exclusions.
- When evaluating the financial strength of an insurer.
- When coordinating insurance with an investment portfolio and estate plans.

## When NOT to use

- For the investment policy itself (use ac-investment-policy-statement).
- For tax treatment of premiums or proceeds (use ac-trading-records-and-tax).
- For assessing a bank's creditworthiness (use ac-bank-and-credit-analysis).

## Status and honest limits

Insurance documents are contracts, and contracts are interpreted by courts and adjusters, not by summaries. A coverage decision made without reading the exclusions is a decision about a product that may not exist at the moment of loss. Insurer strength matters as much as coverage: a claim against an impaired carrier is a claim that may not be paid in full.
