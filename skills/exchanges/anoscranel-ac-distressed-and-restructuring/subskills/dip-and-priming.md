# DIP and priming

## Standard

A debtor-in-possession facility is new money lent to a company already in a court process, and it is usually secured by a lien that ranks ahead of the existing secured debt. That priming is what makes the facility lendable: a lender will not advance funds into a process without priority, so the court is asked to grant it, and existing secured lenders can object but rarely prevent it when the alternative is liquidation.

The arithmetic that matters to an existing holder is what priming does to the cushion. If the new facility and its roll-up consume the equity value above a lien, the previously secured claim can move from money-good to partially secured within the process, and that shift happens before any plan is proposed. The facility's terms — its size, its roll-up of existing debt, and its milestones — are therefore as important as the plan itself.

Primary sources: <https://www.uscourts.gov/> and <https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/>.

## Decision rules

- Record the facility size, the lien priority granted, and whether it primes existing secured debt.
- Record any roll-up of existing debt into the new facility and the effect on the old lenders.
- Record the milestones and the events of default, which set the process timetable.
- Record the interest rate and fees, which are paid from the estate ahead of other claims.
- Record the adequate-protection package offered to existing secured lenders.
- Recompute the secured lender's cushion after the facility.

## Procedure

1. Read the DIP motion and the proposed orders.
2. Record size, priority, and priming.
3. Record the roll-up and its mechanical effect.
4. Record milestones and default events.
5. Recompute the existing lender's collateral coverage.
6. Write the analysis with the cushion before and after.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating the DIP as neutral to existing lenders.
- Ignoring the roll-up, which converts old debt into new priority debt.
- Overlooking milestones that force a sale or a plan by a date.
- Assuming the court will refuse priming because lenders object.
- Forgetting that DIP fees and interest consume estate value first.
