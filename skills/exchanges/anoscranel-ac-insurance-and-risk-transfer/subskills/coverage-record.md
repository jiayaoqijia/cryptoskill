# Coverage record

## Standard

The coverage record is a single document that states, for each risk, whether it is retained or transferred, at what limit, at what cost, and when it was last reviewed. It is the insurance counterpart of the investment policy statement and should be filed with it, so that the household's defences are visible in one place rather than scattered across policies and memory.

Its second purpose is continuity. The person who filed the policies is not always the person who will face the claim, and the record is what lets someone else act. That is why it names the entities, the contacts, and the location of the evidence, not just the limits.

Primary sources: <https://www.naic.org/> and <https://osf.io/prereg/>.

## Decision rules

- The record lists every risk with its classification: retained, transferred, or mandatory.
- Each transferred risk carries the policy number, the limit, the deductible, the premium, and the renewal date.
- Each retained risk carries the reserve that funds it.
- The insurer strength assessment is referenced, not duplicated.
- Review triggers are dated: renewal, asset change, liability change, household change.
- The record names who holds it and where the evidence is.

## Procedure

1. List the risks and their classification.
2. Record the coverage details for transferred risks.
3. Record the reserve for retained risks.
4. Reference the insurer strength assessments.
5. Set the review triggers.
6. File with the investment policy and note the location.

## Anti-patterns

- A list of premiums without limits.
- Omitting retained risks.
- No renewal dates.
- Filing the record only with the policies it summarises.
- No named owner.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Risks listed and classified.
- [ ] Coverage details complete per policy.
- [ ] Reserves recorded for retained risks.
- [ ] Insurer assessments referenced.
- [ ] Review triggers dated.
- [ ] Record filed and owner named.

Keeping the record with the investment policy matters because the two documents answer the same question from opposite ends: what happens if this loss lands. Read together, they show whether the household is funding its risks twice or not at all, and that is the finding the record exists to surface.

Reviewing the record at renewal is easier than reviewing the policies, because the record is one page and the policies are not; the review then decides which policies need reading in full, which is a far better use of attention than re-reading everything every year.
