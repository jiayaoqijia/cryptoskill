# Due diligence questionnaire

## Standard

A due diligence questionnaire is a structured document the manager completes about itself. It covers ownership, team, process, risk controls, service providers, and compliance, and it is useful precisely because it forces the manager to commit to statements that can later be compared with what happens.

Its limits are structural. The answers are self-reported, the questions are set by the industry rather than by the allocator, and the sections that would be most informative — how the process failed, what the manager would do in a scenario it has not experienced, how the team handled a disagreement — are the ones a questionnaire format tends to omit. Reading one means recording what it answers and then asking separately what it does not.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://www.cfainstitute.org/>.

## Decision rules

- Record the DDQ version and the date completed.
- Separate the answers that are verifiable from those that are assertions.
- Record the service providers named, since they can be checked independently.
- Record the sections answered briefly or by reference, and note them as gaps.
- Record the questions the allocator asked outside the DDQ and the answers received.
- Compare this DDQ with the one completed at the previous review, where one exists.

## Procedure

1. Read the DDQ and mark each answer as verifiable or asserted.
2. Record the named service providers.
3. Note the thin answers and by-reference sections.
4. List the questions the form did not ask.
5. Obtain answers to those separately and record them.
6. Write the DDQ assessment with its gaps.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating a completed questionnaire as evidence of quality.
- Accepting a by-reference answer without obtaining the reference.
- Ignoring a DDQ that has not been updated since the last raise.
- Reading the answers without checking the named service providers.
- Assuming the form covers what the allocator needs to know.

## Done checklist

- [ ] DDQ version and date recorded.
- [ ] Answers classified by verifiability.
- [ ] Service providers recorded.
- [ ] Gaps noted.
- [ ] Additional questions asked and recorded.
- [ ] Assessment written.

The most informative section of a completed DDQ is usually the one that describes how the manager's process changed after a loss: a manager that can describe a specific episode, the decision it got wrong, and the change that followed is describing a process that learns, while one that describes only its controls is describing a process that has not been tested. Asking for that narrative outside the form is often worth more than the form itself.
