# Collectibles and opacity

## Standard

A collectible has two prices: what it costs to buy and what it would fetch if you had to sell. Between them sit auction commissions, dealer margins, insurance, storage, and the search time to find a buyer. The gap is large — routinely a fifth or more of the headline value — and it is why collectible returns quoted from indices rarely match what an owner would realise.

The valuation problem is structural. An index of repeat sales measures only the items that sold twice, which is a biased sample of items that were desirable enough to sell. A record price at auction is one transaction between two parties, not a market. Treating either as a mark for a portfolio overstates both the value and the liquidity.

Primary sources: <https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing> and <https://www.sec.gov/investor>.

## Decision rules

- Record the buy price, the all-in cost including commissions and taxes, and the estimated sale price net of the same.
- Record the time-to-sale estimate and the venue that would sell it.
- Treat auction records and index levels as evidence, never as marks.
- Record carrying costs: insurance, storage, maintenance.
- Cap the position size at a level where a total loss is survivable, and state the cap.
- Do not include collectibles in a liquidity plan without a stated haircut.

## Procedure

1. Record buy price and all-in acquisition cost.
2. Estimate net sale proceeds after venue commissions and taxes.
3. Estimate time-to-sale and the venue.
4. Record annual carrying costs.
5. State the position cap and the reason.
6. Write the record with the haircut applied to any liquidity plan.

## Anti-patterns

- Using an auction record as the value of a similar item.
- Forgetting the buyer's premium and the seller's commission.
- Counting a collection as liquid without a haircut.
- Omitting insurance and storage from the return arithmetic.
- Sizing the position as if the loss were partial.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Buy price and all-in cost recorded.
- [ ] Net sale proceeds estimated.
- [ ] Time-to-sale and venue recorded.

The honest test for a collection as an investment is whether it could be sold inside a stated period at a stated haircut. If the answer requires an auction calendar or a buyer who is not yet known, the asset is a possession with a price, not a liquid holding, and the record should say so.
