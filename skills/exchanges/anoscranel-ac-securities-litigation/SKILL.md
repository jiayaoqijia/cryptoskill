# Securities litigation

## Overview

A securities class action is a claim that investors were harmed by a misstatement or an omission, and it proceeds as a collective action in which one lead plaintiff represents the class. Most settle, the settlement creates a fund, and the fund is distributed to class members who file claims. The mechanics determine who receives money and how much, and they are the part that holders most often ignore.

This skill covers those mechanics and the arithmetic around them: how a class is defined, how a claim is calculated, what opting out means, how damages are estimated, how litigation funding changes the incentives, and how insurance and indemnification interact with the claim. It is not legal advice, and it does not predict outcomes.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://www.ftc.gov/>.

## When to use

- When a securities class action has been filed involving a holding.
- When a settlement has been announced and a claim form must be considered.
- When the decision to opt out is being evaluated.
- When a damages estimate or a litigation funding arrangement needs reading.

## When NOT to use

- For the company's credit or equity analysis (use ac-equity-research).
- For insurance policy interpretation in general (use ac-insurance-and-risk-transfer).
- For designing a compliance programme (use ac-security-standards).

## Status and honest limits

Litigation outcomes are uncertain and the estimates are contested by both sides; a damages figure in a complaint is a claim, not a valuation. Jurisdictional rules differ substantially, and a procedure that applies in one forum may not exist in another. Nothing here is legal advice, and a decision with material money at stake belongs with counsel who can read the specific case documents.
