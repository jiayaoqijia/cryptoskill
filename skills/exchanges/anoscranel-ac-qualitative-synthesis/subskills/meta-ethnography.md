# Meta-ethnography

## Standard

Meta-ethnography is the most interpretive approach: it treats each primary study as an account built from participants' accounts, and it produces a new account built from those accounts. The primary studies' concepts — their second-order constructs, the interpretations their authors made of participant first-order accounts — are translated into one another. Where a concept in one study illuminates or reshapes a concept in another, the synthesis is doing its work; where a genuinely new concept emerges that is true to all the accounts but reducible to none, that is a third-order construct, the synthesis's own finding.

This interpretive depth is also its risk. Every step away from the participants' words is a place where the synthesist can substitute their own view for the studies'. The discipline is the constant check of translation against the primary studies' own terms — each third-order construct must be accountable to the second-order constructs that produced it, and each of those to the participant accounts it interpreted.

Primary sources: <https://handbook-5-1.cochrane.org/chapter_9> and <https://www.biomedcentral.com/methods>.

## Decision rules

- Require adequate interpretive depth in the primary studies; meta-ethnography cannot exceed the studies' own interpretive level.
- Identify each study's key second-order constructs before comparing studies.
- Record translations as explicit statements of how one study's concepts relate to another's.
- Build third-order constructs only where a new concept is needed and is true to the accounts.
- Keep a line of sight from every third-order construct to the second-order and first-order material that supports it.
- State where the synthesis line of argument goes beyond the primary accounts, if it does.

## Procedure

1. Extract each study's first-order and second-order constructs with their source text.
2. Compare constructs across studies; record the translations explicitly.
3. Determine the form of synthesis: reciprocal, refuting, or a line of argument.
4. Build third-order constructs where translation demands them.
5. Verify each third-order construct against its supporting second-order and first-order text.
6. State the line of argument and where it stands on the evidence's shoulders.

## Anti-patterns

- Building third-order constructs that cannot be traced to the studies' own concepts.
- Treating participants' words as data to be paraphrased freely.
- Presenting the synthesist's interpretation as a participant's account.
- Selecting studies that support a line of argument already favoured.
- Skipping the depth requirement: thin studies cannot be translated into a line of argument.

## Done checklist

- [ ] First- and second-order constructs extracted with sources.
- [ ] Translations recorded explicitly.
- [ ] Synthesis form chosen and stated.
- [ ] Third-order constructs traced downward.
- [ ] Line of sight maintained from third to first order.
- [ ] Line of argument stated with its dependence on the primary accounts.
