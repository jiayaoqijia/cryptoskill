# Letters of credit

## Standard

A letter of credit is a bank's undertaking to pay against documents, not against goods. The bank examines the documents against the credit's terms, and it pays if they comply and refuses if they do not, regardless of whether the cargo is sound. That separation is the instrument's strength and its weakness: it makes payment predictable, and it makes the documents the only thing that matters.

The terms that decide outcomes are the document list, the description of the goods, the shipment dates, and the tolerance on quantity and value. A discrepancy in any of them permits the bank to refuse, and discrepancies are common enough that a substantial share of presentations are rejected on first examination. Reading a credit means reading the document list and the deadlines as carefully as the price.

Primary sources: <https://www.icc-ccs.org/> and <https://www.bis.org/>.

## Decision rules

- Record the credit type: irrevocable, revocable, confirmed, or transferable.
- Record the document list in full, including any inspection certificate.
- Record the goods description and the tolerance on quantity and amount.
- Record the shipment, presentation, and expiry dates separately.
- Record who confirms the credit and whose risk is being taken.
- Record the discrepancy rules and the practice governing examination.

## Procedure

1. Record the credit terms and its type.
2. Record the document list and the description.
3. Record the dates and the tolerances.
4. Record confirmation and the confirming bank.
5. Record the examination practice and the discrepancy process.
6. Write the credit summary with the payment triggers.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating the credit as a guarantee of the goods.
- Reading the price and not the document list.
- Confusing the presentation deadline with the expiry.
- Assuming confirmation removes all risk rather than the issuing bank's.
- Ignoring a tolerance that changes the amount payable.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Credit type recorded.
- [ ] Document list recorded.
- [ ] Goods description and tolerance recorded.
- [ ] Dates recorded separately.
- [ ] Confirmation recorded.
- [ ] Summary written with triggers.

The presentation deadline is where credits most often fail in practice: documents that are correct but arrive after the stated period are discrepant, and the period runs from the shipment date rather than from the credit's issue, so a delayed shipment compresses the time available to prepare documents. Recording both dates, and the interval between them, converts a compliance requirement into a scheduling constraint.
