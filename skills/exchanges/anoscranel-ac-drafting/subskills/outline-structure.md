# Outline & structure

## Standard

- **Answer first (Minto's pyramid principle).** State the finding in the first sentence, group the supporting arguments so they do not overlap, evidence at the base. Recorded failure mode: losing submissions open with background and bury the recommendation deep in the document.
- **Action titles.** A heading states the point the section proves. "Staffing Approach" tells nothing; "A dedicated senior lead removes the first-month risk" tells the argument. Reading only the headings must reconstruct the case.
- **BLUF plus a criteria-ordered body.** Section order follows the reader's published criteria where any exist. Each section opens with a topic sentence. Acronyms are defined on first use in every major section, because reviewers read out of order.
- **The summary carries everything.** Unassigned readers score without reading the body; the abstract or executive summary must stand alone.
- **Outlining evidence, with its limits.** Outlining improved the product but not the efficiency of writing, by freeing composition time for translation rather than planning (Kellogg 1988, https://doi.apa.org/doi/10.1037/0278-7393.14.2.355).
 The gains attach to inexperienced writers: "experienced authors writing routine documents are not likely to gain much from outlining."
 No study tests outline-first against prose-first on scientific or technical manuscripts (<https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.15067>, null N6 ⚠️).

## Decision rules

1. Write the one-sentence answer before any heading. If it cannot be written, the research is not finished.
2. Group sections so no two overlap. Overlap means two headings claim one scope.
3. Order by the reader's decision, not by chronology or by your process.
4. Every heading passes the action-title test: it states a point, not a label.
5. Depth is two levels. A third level means the section splits or merges.
6. Routine short documents (an email, a note) may skip the outline and go to draft (subskills/drafting.md).
7. A venue's published order (rubric, form, template) overrides your preferred order.

## Procedure

1. Write the one-sentence answer at the top of the file.
2. List the questions the reader asks, in the order they ask them. These become the sections.
3. Draft headings as full claims. Read the heading list alone: it must carry the argument.
4. Under each heading, write the topic sentence and the evidence classes it needs (number, case, quote, figure).
5. Check coverage: every brief requirement has a section; every section serves a brief requirement.
6. Check the boundary: no section repeats another. Merge or cut.
7. Cut sections that only exist to show effort. Record what went.

## Anti-patterns

- Opening with history or company overview when the reader wants the answer.
- Headings as topic labels ("Overview", "Details", "Conclusion").
- Chronological order for a decision document.
- Sections sized by the writing, not by the reader's question.
- A summary that does not carry the argument alone.

## Done checklist

- [ ] One-sentence answer written and placed first.
- [ ] Headings state points; the heading list alone carries the argument.
- [ ] Section order follows the reader's decision or published criteria.
- [ ] No two sections overlap.
- [ ] Topic sentence and evidence type under every heading.
- [ ] Cut sections recorded.
