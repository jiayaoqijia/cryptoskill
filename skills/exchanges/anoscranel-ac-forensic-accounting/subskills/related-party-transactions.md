# Related-party transactions

## Standard

A related-party transaction is a dealing between the company and someone connected to it: a director, a major shareholder, an entity those parties control, or a family member. Disclosure rules require these to be reported, and the disclosure typically names the party, the amount, and the nature of the relationship. Reading them is straightforward; the questions come from what is not disclosed.

The gaps worth documenting are structural. A counterparty whose ownership is described only as "affiliated with" a director; a transaction that recurs annually without competitive process; a balance that grows faster than the business; and a relationship that appears in one year's filing and not the next without explanation. Each is a question to put to the document, not a conclusion about the company.

Primary sources: <https://www.sec.gov/investor> and <https://www.nber.org/>.

## Decision rules

- Record every related party named and the relationship described.
- Record the transaction amount, its nature, and whether it is recurring.
- Record whether the terms are stated to be at arm's length and on what basis.
- Record any balance outstanding to or from the related party and its trend.
- Record counterparties whose ownership is described vaguely.
- Record disclosures that appear in one period and not the next.

## Procedure

1. Collect the related-party note across several periods.
2. Record parties, amounts, and stated relationships.
3. Record recurring transactions and their trend.
4. Record outstanding balances and their growth.
5. Identify vague ownership descriptions and missing periods.
6. Write the questions with their document references.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating disclosure as proof of impropriety.
- Ignoring a related-party note because the amounts are small relative to revenue.
- Overlooking balances rather than flows.
- Assuming a disclosed relationship explains an undisclosed one.
- Reading one year's note in isolation.

## Done checklist

- [ ] Parties and relationships recorded.
- [ ] Amounts and nature recorded.
- [ ] Arm's-length statements recorded.
- [ ] Balances and trends recorded.
- [ ] Vague ownership flagged.
- [ ] Questions documented with references.

The test that separates routine from notable is whether a transaction has a counterparty with an independent reason to trade: a lease to a director's company at a market rent is unremarkable, while a purchase from an entity with no other customers and no visible operations is a question the disclosure itself has raised by existing.
