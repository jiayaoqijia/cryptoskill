# Investment oversight and OCIO

## Overview

When investment decisions are delegated, the delegator keeps a duty to oversee them. That duty is not satisfied by receiving reports; it requires a structure: a mandate that states what the manager may and may not do, a monitoring process that tests compliance with it, reporting that a board can act on, and a record that shows the oversight happened. An oversight function without those is a signature on an annual review.

This skill covers that structure, including the outsourced model in which a single provider takes the investment function as well as the implementation. Outsourcing concentrates the decisions but not the responsibility, and it creates conflicts — the provider's own funds, its fee schedule, its discretion over manager selection — that the oversight function has to see through rather than around.

Primary sources: <https://www.cfainstitute.org/> and <https://www.sec.gov/investor>.

## When to use

- When investment decisions are delegated to a manager or an OCIO.
- When a mandate, guideline, or policy needs to be monitored rather than written.
- When board or committee reporting needs to be designed or reviewed.
- When the oversight function's own evidence needs testing.

## When NOT to use

- For evaluating a manager's operations, fees, and terms (use ac-manager-and-vehicle-diligence).
- For drafting the policy that oversight tests against (use ac-investment-policy-statement).
- For computing returns and attribution (use ac-performance-measurement).

## Status and honest limits

Governance documents describe intent, and oversight is the evidence that intent was carried out. A policy that has never been tested, a mandate that has never been breached, and a report that has never prompted a question are three signs of a function that exists on paper. Nothing here recommends a manager or a model, and the fiduciary standard that applies depends on the jurisdiction and the entity type.
